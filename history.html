Our Roots • Our Identity • Our Legacy

The History
of Amanokrom

Discover the origins, traditional institutions, spiritual heritage and enduring story of Amanokrom in the Akuapem traditional area of Ghana.

Our Story

A Heritage Rooted in Courage and Tradition

The history of Amanokrom is preserved through oral traditions, ancestral accounts, traditional leadership, sacred places and community customs. These stories connect generations and help preserve the identity and heritage of the people.

Wopɛ ko! Wopɛ ko! Wopɛ ko!
Ɔko yɛ me adedade.

“You love war! You love war! You love war! War is my nature. War is my life.”

Traditional war chant attributed to Gyasehene Abentia (Horn).

Chapter One • The Beginning

The Founding of Amanokrom

According to the community's oral history, Amanokrom was founded around 1742 through the coming together of Nana Amanor Awuah of the Asona Abusua and Konton Mensah of the Denkyira Agona lineage.

Nana Amanor Awuah's ancestral traditions are connected with Adanse Ofoase, Nana Ahenkorakese and historical movements associated with Nana Safori and the formation of the Akuapem political structure in the early eighteenth century.

These traditions also preserve connections with the Ofori Stool Dynasty and ancestral movements through Pamen in Akyem Abuakwa.

Konton Mensah's family history is associated with Dodowa and Amanoakura. The meeting of these lineages forms an important part of the community's founding story.

Migration and Settlement

From Akropong to Amanokrom

Oral accounts recount a dispute around 1740 during the Ohum Festival involving regent Ofei Boa and Nana Amanor Awuah. The disagreement is remembered as part of the circumstances that led Nana Amanor Awuah to leave Akropong.

According to these accounts, he travelled towards Akyem Abuakwa, passing through Adesaase and Odumase near Dodowa. There he encountered Konton Mensah, and the two lineages established a settlement that became known as Amanokrom.

The settlement's history reflects migration, family alliances and the development of traditional authority in the area.

Traditional Authority

The Gyaase of Okuapemman

The Gyaase occupies an important place in the traditional organisation and ceremonial life of Okuapemman. Its responsibilities are remembered through customary duties, community institutions and traditional observances.

Local traditions associate the Osomanyawo family, linked with Konton Mensah, with spiritual responsibilities and important roles in the traditional life of Amanokrom.

Amanokrom's historical identity also reflects the relationships among its Asona, Agona, Bratu, Oyoko and other families.

Courage and Spiritual Heritage

The Akatamansu War and Senyani Abena

The Akatamansu War forms part of the historical traditions associated with the ancestors of Amanokrom and the wider Akuapem area. Stories of warfare are preserved alongside accounts of courage, spiritual protection and traditional responsibilities.

Senyani Abena is remembered as an important deity in these traditions. Its associated saying is:

Se wo nya no a, womin.

“Once it possesses you, you cannot escape.”

Oral accounts describe rituals involving sacred fire, the jumping of flames and a sacred pot containing water and leaves. The traditions also refer to sacred regalia and a shrine location associated with the area where the Presbyterian Junior High School now stands.

Sacred Traditions

Adenkyemsu Kofi and Oboansa

Adenkyemsu Kofi is remembered among the traditional spiritual figures associated with the area's heritage. Oboansa, also referred to in connection with the Sika River, is linked in local tradition with Senyani Abena.

Local accounts describe Oboansa as flowing from Amanokrom through Rudurudu towards Dodowa.

Protection and Belief

Kyenku and Obosomase

Kyenku, also known in the tradition as Kyenku Bayaw, is associated with Obosomase and remembered as a wind deity believed to protect warriors.

These traditions reflect the place of spiritual beliefs in the historical memory of communities across the Akuapem hills.

Community Organisation

The Asafo Companies and Their Symbols

Asafo institutions have played important roles in traditional organisation, public ceremonies and the collective identity of the community. Traditional accounts mention the following symbols and instruments:

  • Darunta: the double gongon.
  • Abogyese: the war drum.
  • Frankaa: the war flag.

Groups remembered in local accounts include Ankyease, Bretu, Porebon and Twafo, with Twafo associated with traditional scouting duties.

Movement and Commerce

Historical Trade Routes

Amanokrom's location connected it to routes leading through Adawso, Mpeko, Obotra and Abonse. These routes formed part of wider networks through which people, goods and information moved between inland communities and the coast.

Community accounts also refer to routes from the area near the Presbyterian Junior High School, along the River Rudurudu and through Brumanso towards Lower Dodowa.

Some oral accounts describe the detention of enslaved people in caves at Abonse before they were taken towards coastal markets. This history should be remembered as part of the painful history of enslavement and the trade in human beings.

Oral History

Okomfo Anokye and the Oware Tradition

A local tradition connects Okomfo Anokye with the making or playing of Oware on a sacred stone using his heels. The account describes marbles appearing and disappearing in a manner remembered as miraculous.

The saying “Asutewa 3so Aguare” is preserved in connection with this tradition. It expresses the idea that a community may be small in number yet remain resourceful, self-sufficient and difficult to defeat.

Customs and Taboos

Tuesday Traditions

Tuesday is remembered as a taboo day in the community's traditional calendar. Oral accounts describe restrictions connected with Nmore, blood sacrifices, the announcement of death or bad news, and the handling of certain agricultural produce and firewood.

These customs form part of the community's traditional understanding of respect, observance and spiritual responsibility.

Sacred Waters

Fridays and Fofie

Friday, known in this tradition as Fofie, is associated with purification practices involving the palace of Okofrobour, Atrankogua households and the wider community.

Tradition forbids entering the sacred river on Fridays. A deity is also said in oral accounts to appear in the form of a golden hen accompanied by chicks.

Sacred Heritage

The Sacred Earthenware Pot and Beads

A sacred earthenware pot associated with Konton, Amanokrom and the Osomanyawa tradition holds a special place in local accounts. The pot and its beads are traditionally believed to be connected with healing, fertility, strength and prosperity.

These beliefs form part of the community's spiritual heritage and the oral traditions passed between generations.

Heritage Today

Preserving the Legacy of Amanokrom

Today, Amanokrom continues to honour its history through traditional leadership, cultural observances, family histories and community life. Its heritage remains an important part of the identity of its people.

The present Stool is occupied by Oyeeman Okofrobo Osim Kwatia II, representing the continuing traditional leadership of Amanokrom.

Preserving the community's history helps future generations understand their roots, respect their elders and carry their cultural heritage forward.
